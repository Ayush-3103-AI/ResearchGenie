# How does urban green space access affect residents' mental health?

## Introduction

The relationship between urban green space access and residents' mental health is a central question in environmental psychology and public health. While broad consensus exists that green spaces confer benefits, the evidence base reveals significant heterogeneity in outcomes, mechanisms, and study quality. This review synthesizes findings across three primary domains: environmental factors, outdoor activities, and social cohesion, while critically examining methodological constraints and contextual moderators.

The literature identifies three distinct pathways through which green space influences mental health. Environmental factors operate primarily at the neighborhood scale, where improved air quality, noise attenuation, and visual stimulation reduce physiological stress and enhance sleep quality in adults [1]. Outdoor activities facilitated by parks and urban forests provide venues for physical exercise, which is a well-established determinant of mental well-being [1]. Social cohesion emerges through green spaces that foster communication and community engagement, particularly benefiting children and the elderly who may have limited mobility or social networks elsewhere [1]. These pathways are not mutually exclusive; rather, they often interact synergistically, with greener environments correlating with higher levels of reported happiness and social satisfaction, especially in densely populated urban settings [1].

However, the strength and direction of these associations vary considerably based on individual and contextual characteristics. Heterogeneity is evident across age groups, gender, socioeconomic status, and urban development levels [1]. For instance, longitudinal evidence suggests that moving to greener areas can yield sustained improvements in mental health scores, supporting a 'shifting baseline' hypothesis where environmental enrichment provides lasting benefits [2]. Conversely, in low- and middle-income countries with inadequate sanitation infrastructure, green spaces may inadvertently increase exposure to infectious diseases, potentially offsetting mental health gains [1]. This underscores the necessity of considering local context, including ecological health functions and tree species composition, when evaluating green space interventions [1].

Despite these promising findings, establishing causal relationships remains challenging. The majority of studies rely on cross-sectional designs that preclude temporal precedence between exposure and outcome, allowing confounding variables such as socioeconomic status to distort observed associations [3]. Furthermore, measurement error is pervasive; many studies depend on subjective self-reports of both environmental access and psychological states, which may not align with objective conditions [3]. Sampling biases further limit generalizability, as research often focuses on young adults in the global North who have internet access, excluding vulnerable populations and those in different demographic contexts [3].

Methodological inconsistencies compound these issues. Terms like 'green space' are frequently used without precise definitions, leading to mixed results when examining outcomes such as depression or stress [4]. Many studies employ simple mean comparisons without adjusting for confounders or utilize non-representative samples like university students [4]. Additionally, mediators such as loneliness or stress often overlap with mental health measurements themselves, complicating causal inference [1]. The duration of green space use is sometimes incorrectly treated as a mediator when it functions more appropriately as an independent variable reflecting usage frequency [1].

Future research must address these limitations by adopting standardized metrics for green space exposure and employing longitudinal or randomized controlled designs to clarify causal mechanisms [1][4]. Policy implications suggest that urban planning should be demand-oriented, integrating diverse green space types while accounting for residents' socioeconomic characteristics and local environmental conditions [1]. Recognizing the multifaceted benefits of green spaces—including biodiversity preservation, temperature regulation, and well-being enhancement—is crucial for guiding sustainable urban development practices worldwide [5]. However, simplistic interventions that focus solely on landscape redesign may fail to address underlying determinants of urban health that require broader structural changes [6].

In sum, while urban green space access generally promotes mental health through environmental, behavioral, and social pathways, the magnitude and consistency of these effects depend on a complex interplay of individual traits, group characteristics, and contextual factors. The current evidence base, though robust in identifying positive trends, is constrained by methodological weaknesses that limit causal inference and generalizability.
